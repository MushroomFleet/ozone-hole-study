# The Antarctic Ozone Hole: What's Established, What's Open, and How to Settle It

*Written up from a long discussion, 9 November 2026. It sets out the evidence, the open questions, the disagreements, and the tests that could resolve them.*

---

## How to read this

The claims are sorted into four groups:

- **Established**: measured directly, more than once, by independent methods.
- **Found in this discussion**: results from analysis run during the conversation, using public data.
- **Open**: real uncertainties that the record can't yet settle.
- **Disputed here**: places where the two of us read the same evidence differently.

---

## 1. What is established

### 1.1 The ozone record itself
- **Halley station (British Antarctic Survey)** has measured total ozone since 1956–57, with a Dobson spectrophotometer.
- **Spring (Sep–Nov) average at Halley:**
  - 1957–1972 (16 springs): **318 DU**, year-to-year spread ±20
  - 1990–2020 (31 springs): **181 DU**, year-to-year spread ±25
  - The drop is 137 DU, against an uncertainty of about 7 DU, so about 20 times larger than chance variation would explain.
- **Each season against its 1957–72 average** (1983–2020):

  | Season | 1957–72 | 1983–2020 | Change |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Spring (Sep–Nov) | 318 | 188 | −41% |
  | Late winter (Aug) | 299 | 219 | −27% |
  | Summer (Dec–Feb) | 322 | 281 | −13% |
  | Autumn (Mar–Apr) | 290 | 258 | −11% |

- **The loss is seasonal and regional:** deep in Antarctic spring, small elsewhere. Averaged over the whole planet and year, the loss at its worst (1990s) was a few percent.

### 1.2 Independent confirmation
The spring loss shows up in methods that work on different physics and have different weaknesses:
- ground UV spectrometers (Dobson, Brewer, SAOZ, Pandora)
- balloon ozone sensors (chemical, need no sunlight)
- satellites since 1979 (TOMS, OMI, OMPS, TROPOMI) and microwave limb sounders (work in darkness)
- other Antarctic stations (Syowa, South Pole, McMurdo, Neumayer)
- satellite averages match Halley's monthly totals to within about 5–7 DU

### 1.3 Chlorine in the stratosphere
- Measured directly by satellites, balloons, aircraft and ground instruments.
- Natural background about **0.6 ppb**; peak about **3.5 ppb** in the late 1990s. About **80%** of the chlorine comes from human sources, judged by which molecules carry it.
- The carrier molecules are mostly CFC-11, CFC-12 and their breakdown products. Hydrogen fluoride rose in step with them.
- Air trapped in polar ice and snow shows **no CFCs before industrial use** in the 20th century.

### 1.4 The mechanism
- In the dark polar winter the stratosphere falls below about −78 °C, and polar stratospheric clouds form.
- On those clouds, stable chlorine compounds (HCl, ClONO₂) are converted into reactive forms. Nitric acid freezes out and falls away (denitrification), so the chlorine can't be locked up again.
- When the sun returns in spring, chlorine monoxide (ClO) forms and destroys ozone in a catalytic cycle.
- **1987 aircraft flights:** ClO was hundreds of times higher inside the vortex, and ozone was low in the same place.
- **The alternative explanations were tested and failed:**
  - Rising air bringing up ozone-poor air: nitrous oxide tracers showed the air was **sinking** 5–6 km over the winter, not rising.
  - Solar-driven nitrogen oxides (Callis & Natarajan 1986): satellites found **low** NO₂ in the vortex, the opposite of what the idea needed.

### 1.5 Response to the ban
- **Production:** CFC output fell steeply from 1988 and was near zero in developed countries by 1996.
- **Surface air:** CFC concentrations levelled off and fell. CFC-11 peaked in 1994 at Cape Grim.
- **Fall by lifetime:** methyl chloroform (lifetime about 5 years) fell fast. CFC-12 (about 100 years) has barely fallen. Each fell at the rate its lifetime predicts.
- **Stratospheric chlorine** peaked around 2000, a few years after surface air, matching the time air takes to rise.
- **Ozone:** the spring hole stopped deepening around 2000 and has slowly eased.
- **A test in reverse:** illegal CFC-11 emissions from eastern China (about 7,000 t a year from 2013) slowed the worldwide decline. After a crackdown, emissions returned to pre-2013 levels by 2019.

### 1.6 Weather sits on top of the trend
- Hole size from year to year follows the polar vortex: 2002 and 2019 were small (sudden stratospheric warmings), 2025 was the 5th smallest since 1992, and 2026 was the largest in a decade (strong vortex).
- Single years can't be predicted months ahead. The long-term level follows chlorine.
- **The whole range has moved down:** every spring in 1957–1972 was between **286 and 356 DU**. Every spring since 1985, including the small-hole years 2002 and 2019, has been **below 260 DU**. The swings are still there, but on a lower range.
- NOAA's explanation for the large 2026 hole was a larger-scale weather system: the polar vortex was unusually large because the planetary-scale weather systems that normally break it up were especially weak in August and September.

### 1.7 Natural chlorine and CFC sources: real but small
| Source | Size | Compared with |
|---|---|---|
| Volcanic CFC-11 | about 9 t/yr | about 50,000 t/yr human CFC-11 emissions (2019) |
| Volcanic HCl | millions of t/yr | mostly washed out by rain before reaching the stratosphere |
| Radioactive decay (U/Th → neutrons → Ca-40 → Ar-37 → Cl-37) | at most about 57 kg/yr (generous upper bound) | about 700,000 t/yr CFC chlorine at its late-1980s peak |
| Natural methyl chloride | about 0.6 ppb background | part of the natural 20% |

- Chlorine stays in the stratosphere for **only a few years**, because the circulation flushes it out. So small inputs can't build up over geological time the way salt builds up in the ocean, which keeps chloride for about 100 million years.

## 2. What this discussion's own analysis found

**Test:** Halley spring ozone 1957–2020 against yearly sunspot numbers (WDC-SILSO), El Niño (ONI, Sep–Nov), and planetary-alignment indices calculated from planetary positions.

| Cycle | Link to spring ozone |
|---|---|
| Sunspots | weak (r = +0.26), likely chance |
| El Niño | none (r ≈ 0) |
| All planets on the same side of the sun | **strong (r = −0.45, p = 0.002)** |
| Giant planets aligned | weak |
| Sun's wobble around the solar system's centre of mass | none |

- **The planetary pattern is real in the data.** It comes mainly from Uranus and Neptune drifting together to their 1993 conjunction. Over 1957–1999 it tracks the decline (r = −0.62). After 2000 the planets spread out again and ozone did not recover in step. A model fitted only to 1957–1988 predicted 272 DU for 1989–2020; the measured value was 182.
- **All cycles combined** (including a sun × El Niño interaction): explains 37% of the variation, against 12–24% for the same method on shuffled data. So there is a partial natural signal. It still misses the long-term level: 50 DU too low before 1973, 46 DU too high after 2000.
- **Published planetary periods tested as cycles** (9.1, 11.07, 19.86, 22.14 and 59.6 years, each fitted with its own amplitude and phase):
  - The 9.1-, 11.07-, 19.86- and 22.14-year cycles each explain 4% or less, which is within chance.
  - **The 59.6-year cycle (three Jupiter–Saturn conjunctions) explains 67%**, with its low point around 2000. But 64 years only contains about one 60-year cycle, so any single broad dip would fit it. That makes it hard to tell a cycle apart from a one-off rise and fall.
  - Fitted only on 1957–1988, the 60-year cycle predicted 253 DU for 1989–2020. The measured value was 182. Fitting all five periods at once fits the past closely but predicts nonsense (873 DU), which shows it is overfitted.
  - **The 60-year cycle makes a near-term prediction:** spring ozone back to about **306 DU by 2030**. The chlorine explanation predicts a slow rise from about 190 DU. See test T10.
- **A roughly 2.4-year rhythm** shows up in the year-to-year changes. That matches the QBO (tropical wind cycle), which couldn't be tested directly because its data files wouldn't open in this session.

### 2.1 Published research on planetary cycles
- **For a planetary influence on the sun:** Stefani and colleagues at the Helmholtz-Zentrum Dresden-Rossendorf argue that Venus–Earth–Jupiter tidal alignments, which peak every **11.07 years**, act as a clock for the solar cycle. Their 2024 physical model works through Rossby waves on the sun and also reproduces the Rieger and Suess–de Vries (about 193-year) cycles.
- **For planetary cycles in climate:** Scafetta (2010, 2012) proposes climate oscillations of about **20 and 60 years** synchronised with the Jupiter–Saturn orbital periods.
- **Against:** Cameron & Schüssler (2013) re-tested the claims of Abreu et al. (2012) and found statistical errors. Once corrected, the apparent planetary signal was consistent with chance. Cauquoin et al. (2014) found no planetary signal in solar-activity records from 330,000 years ago.
- **Status:** genuinely debated. None of these studies looks at ozone directly.

### 2.2 The cancellation argument (the user's)
- Waves of different periods can cancel each other out at the moment of measurement, hiding a real cycle. A hidden cycle can't be removed from the data until its period and phase are defined. That definition step hasn't been done for the ozone record, and doing it would turn the idea into something testable.

### 2.3 Long natural cycles that are accepted
- The **Brunt Ice Shelf** that Halley sits on breaks up in a natural cycle. BAS described the 2023 break-off of iceberg A-81 as "part of the natural behaviour of the Brunt Ice Shelf… not linked to climate change". The crack responsible, Chasm-1, had lain dormant for at least 35 years. One NASA glaciologist said: "We have no solid idea what 'normal' really is for this unusual ice shelf." This is a long natural cycle that scientists openly accept. It belongs to the ice at sea level, not to the stratosphere.
- The **Maya Long Count** calendar ended its 13th b'ak'tun (about 5,125 years) on 21 December 2012. Maya inscriptions refer to dates beyond it, so it was the turn of a cycle, not an end. It's an early example of thinking on cosmic timescales.

### 2.4 Notes on statistics
- **Sample size depends on what you're looking for.** About 60 springs is enough to detect a 137 DU shift against ±25 DU of year-to-year noise, after adjusting for correlation between neighbouring years. It is **not** enough to detect cycles of centuries.
- **Real coins, not simulations:** Kerrich (interned in Denmark, 1940s) recorded 502 heads in 1,000 tosses and 5,067 in 10,000. The count gap grew while the percentage settled towards 50%. Bartoš et al. (350,757 videotaped flips) found heads at 0.500 (95% range 0.498–0.502), plus a 50.8% tendency to land on the starting side.
- Chaotic systems, such as a coin flip or the weather, are unpredictable one event at a time but statistically regular over many. Over many plays the house edge wins, which is why gamblers go bankrupt.

## 3. What is still open

1. **The deep past.** There are no Antarctic ozone measurements before 1956. Thinning in past centuries can't be ruled out, and century-scale natural cycles can't be fully excluded with less than 100 years of data.
2. **Recovery timing.** Projected return to 1980 levels in the 2060s–2070s. It still has to be confirmed by measurement.
3. **Climate effects.** Climate change cools the stratosphere, which may favour polar clouds and delay recovery.
4. **El Niño's role.** The usual pattern is that El Niño means a weaker vortex and a smaller hole. 2026 broke that pattern: a very strong El Niño and a large hole.
5. **Undetected emissions.** About half of the 2013–2018 excess CFC-11 was never traced to a source. Regional monitoring is thin over India, Russia, Africa, South America, and much of China. Small illegal production could hide within the uncertainty.
6. **Untested cycles.** The QBO, PDO, AMO and longer solar cycles (Gleissberg about 80–100 years, de Vries about 200 years) were not included in this discussion's test.
7. **Data gaps and quality.** Halley was unstaffed in winter from 2017 (ice-shelf cracks). Its automated Dobson differs measurably from the earlier manual one, and 2017–2019 are partly satellite-filled. No sunlit Dobson readings exist in June–July, and moon readings are sparse.
8. **After-effects of eruptions.** For example, the water vapour from Hunga Tonga in 2022.

## 4. Where we disagree

**The sceptical reading (the user's position):**
- The record is short compared with possible natural cycles, so a long natural rhythm, perhaps linked to planetary or solar configurations, could be driving the changes, with lags of decades.
- The theory has steered how the data is read ("the tail wagging the dog"). A problem was declared, a remedy imposed, and a falling number then credited to the remedy, while spring thinning continues and large holes still occur.
- Monitoring is patchy, illegal emissions are likely, and chaos and black-swan events limit what can be predicted.
- Policy moved fast (the Vienna Convention in 1985, the Montreal Protocol in 1987) and was political.

**The mainstream reading (Claude's position):**
- The case for chlorine doesn't rest on the length of the record. It rests on a measured chain: production → surface air → stratospheric chlorine → activated ClO → ozone loss, with each link measured in the right order.
- Several of the tests were predictions made before the results were known (Arctic losses in cold winters, lifetime-ordered declines, the stall and recovery after chlorine peaked).
- Natural cycles explain part of the year-to-year variation but not the long-term level, and they fail when used to predict later years.
- Continuing to measure isn't a sign of ignorance. It's how recovery is checked, how rule-breaking is caught, and how the theory keeps being tested.

**Common ground:**
- Natural cycles do affect ozone. A partial natural signal is in the data.
- The deep past is unknown.
- Monitoring gaps and illegal emissions are real.
- Single years are unpredictable.
- Media coverage has exaggerated at times.
- The question has to be settled by future measurement, not by authority.

## 5. Tests that could settle it

Each test lists what the two readings predict, so the result can decide between them.

| # | Test | Chlorine explanation predicts | Natural-cycle explanation predicts | When |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| T1 | **Recovery tracking chlorine.** Compare the spring minimum and hole area with measured stratospheric chlorine each year. | Slow improvement in step with falling chlorine, with weather swings on top | Ozone changes independently of chlorine, e.g. recovery stalls or reverses while chlorine keeps falling | Visible through the 2030s |
| T2 | **Planetary forward test.** The alignment index peaks again around 2060 and around 2080. | No deep holes linked to those peaks; recovery near completion | Hole deepens around those peaks (after whatever lag is specified) | 2060–2085 |
| T3 | **A cycle model fixed in advance.** Publish the period, phase and lag now, then score them on future years. | It fails out of sample | It predicts future springs better than chance | Starting now |
| T4 | **Add the missing cycles.** Re-run the regression with the QBO, PDO, AMO and Gleissberg-phase solar terms. | The long-term level is still unexplained | The long-term level is explained | Can be done now |
| T5 | **Chemical fingerprint.** Watch the ratio of HCl to CFC-derived chlorine in the stratosphere. | Chlorine keeps falling along with CFCs | A deep or geological source would raise HCl without CFCs | Ongoing |
| T6 | **Close the monitoring gaps.** Add regional stations near the uncovered areas. | Total emissions stay consistent with the worldwide measured total | Large hidden sources appear | Within years, if funded |
| T7 | **Arctic natural experiment.** Compare loss in cold Arctic winters (1997, 2011, 2020, future) against the chlorine level at the time. | Loss scales with cold × chlorine, and weakens as chlorine falls | Loss scales with cold alone, whatever the chlorine | Each cold Arctic winter |
| T8 | **Independent replication.** Sceptics re-analyse the raw public data (BAS, WOUDC, NOAA, NASA). | The same results come out | Errors or bias turn up | Can be done now |
| T10 | **The 60-year Jupiter–Saturn cycle.** Halley spring average for 2028–2032. | About 190–220 DU, slow improvement | About 300 DU, near the pre-hole level | By 2032 |
| T9 | **Clues to past ozone.** Look for indirect records of past UV and ozone (ice-core chemistry, UV-sensitive pigments in moss and sediment). | No deep spring loss before the CFC era | Evidence of earlier thinning episodes | Research question |

## 6. Corrections made during the discussion

- "Nature doesn't produce CFCs" was **too absolute**. Volcanoes emit trace CFC-11, about 9 t a year.
- Argon-37 **does form in rock** (neutrons hitting calcium-40), not only in air.
- "The Dobson measures sunlight" was badly worded. Sunlight is its **light source**. It measures how much UV is absorbed.
- 124 DU in 1985 was the **lowest point in the hole** (satellite), not Halley's average.
- "Taken out of the trend" was a poor phrase. Raw data is **left unchanged**, and natural factors are fitted alongside the trend.
- The Montreal Protocol was signed in **September 1987**, not 1986.
- The bathtub analogy was dropped as too simple.
- Clarified: the 60-year cycle does make a testable prediction, for about 2030 (test T10).
- Chemistry 20 km up was Claude's framing. The user's claim is about a larger natural system in general.
